# Growth stages

Knowing roughly what's happening — and when — takes a lot of the worry out of puppyhood. Just remember the headline: **these ages are approximate, and they stretch out the bigger your dog will be.**

## The developmental stages

### Neonatal & transitional (0–3 weeks)
Before you have them. Eyes and ears closed at first; fully dependent on mom.

### Socialization period (3–14 weeks)
The most important developmental window of a dog's life. The brain is primed to accept new experiences as normal. This is where confidence (or fear) is built — see [Socializing safely](../03-foundations/socialization.md). Most puppies come home at **8–10 weeks**, in the middle of this period.

### Juvenile (3–6 months)
Rapid growth and learning. **Teething** hits (baby teeth out, 42 adult teeth in by ~6–7 months — expect heavy chewing). Bladder control improves. Great age for training basics and continued socialization.

### Adolescence (6–18 months)
Hormones arrive; confidence and independence surge. Your "perfectly trained" puppy may seem to forget everything and test boundaries. Energy peaks. This is the most-surrendered age for exactly this reason — and it's the chapter [Surviving adolescence](adolescence.md) is devoted to. Hang in there; it passes.

### Social maturity / adulthood
When the brain and body finish settling. **This is where size changes everything** (below).

## Maturity by size — the timeline that actually matters

!!! tip "Size note: when is my dog 'grown up'?"
    | Size (adult weight) | Roughly full-grown | Mentally mature |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | **Toy/small** (under ~25 lb) | **9–12 months** | ~12 months |
    | **Medium** (25–50 lb) | **12–15 months** | ~1.5 years |
    | **Large** (50–90 lb) | **15–18 months** | ~2 years |
    | **Giant** (over ~90 lb) | **18–24 months** | ~2–3 years |

    This single fact drives a lot of decisions: how long to feed puppy food,
    when growth plates close (and therefore [spay/neuter
    timing](spay-neuter.md) and safe high-impact exercise), and how long to be
    patient with adolescent goofiness.

## Exercise: enough, but not too much (especially big dogs)

A useful rule of thumb for **structured** exercise is about **5 minutes per month of age, once or twice a day** — so a 4-month-old gets ~20 minutes at a stretch. Free play and sniffing are different and self-limiting.

!!! warning "Protect growing joints"
    For **large- and giant-breed puppies**, avoid **repetitive high-impact
    activity** — long runs on pavement, forced jogging, jumping off furniture,
    intense fetch on hard ground, and stairs in excess — until growth plates
    close (often **12–18+ months**). Too much, too young raises the risk of
    joint problems later. Sniffy walks, gentle play, and mental work are
    always good.

## Weight & body condition over numbers

Don't chase a number on a chart. Use **body condition**: you should be able to **feel the ribs easily** without pressing hard, **see a waist** from above, and see a **tuck-up** in the belly from the side. Keeping a puppy lean — especially a big one — is one of the most protective things you can do for their joints and lifespan. Your vet will track this at each visit.

## Behavior shifts to expect

- **Fear periods:** brief stages (often around ~8–11 weeks and again in adolescence) where puppies suddenly find normal things scary. Don't force it; stay calm, keep experiences positive, and it passes.
- **Independence:** adolescents wander further and "test" recall. Go back to rewarding heavily and use a long line for safety.
- **Energy spikes:** more structured outlets and training prevent boredom-driven trouble.
