# Spay & neuter timing

Whether to spay (females) or neuter (males) is, for most pet owners, a clear yes — it prevents unwanted litters, eliminates some cancers and infections (like the often-fatal uterine infection *pyometra* in females), and tends to be associated with **longer lifespans.** The more nuanced question, and the one this chapter focuses on, is **when** — because timing genuinely affects long-term joint and cancer risk, and the best answer now depends on your dog's **size and breed.**

!!! note "This is a 'decide with your vet' topic"
    The guidance below reflects current U.S. thinking (AAHA life-stage
    guidelines and recent UC Davis breed research), but the right call balances
    health, behavior, your ability to manage an intact dog, and local norms.
    **Make this decision with your veterinarian, who knows your individual
    dog.**

## Why timing changed

For decades the default was "spay/neuter by 6 months." Then research — much of it led by UC Davis — found that for **large and giant breeds**, removing sex hormones *before* the growth plates close can delay their closure, alter bone growth, and raise the risk of **joint disorders (like cruciate ligament tears and hip dysplasia) and certain cancers.** A 2024 UC Davis study expanded breed-specific guidance to dozens of breeds, reinforcing that **early spay/neuter has clearer downsides the bigger the dog.** Small dogs (roughly under 20 lb) don't show the same effects.

## General timing by size

!!! tip "Size note (the headline guidance)"
    | Adult size | Common guidance |
    | --- | --- |
    | **Small/toy** (under ~45 lb projected) | Around **6 months**; females often spayed **before the first heat** (~5–6 months). |
    | **Medium** | Roughly **6–12 months**; discuss whether waiting helps your dog. |
    | **Large/giant** (over ~45–50 lb projected) | Wait for **skeletal maturity — about 12–18 months** — to protect joint development. |

    The AAHA life-stage guidelines put it simply: small-breed dogs are
    typically altered around six months, while large-breed dogs are best
    neutered **after growth stops**, often between **9 and 15 months**, and some
    giant or specific breeds later still.

## Breed matters, not just size

The 2024 UC Davis research gives **breed-specific** windows because risk profiles differ even among large dogs. A few examples from that work:

- **Mastiff:** wait notably longer — around **24 months for males**, **12 months for females.**
- **German Shorthaired/Wirehaired Pointer, Newfoundland:** **no earlier than ~12 months** for both sexes.
- **Rhodesian Ridgeback:** around **6 months** was acceptable in the data.
- **Siberian Husky:** ~**6 months for males**, ~**12 months for females.**

If you have a purebred or know the predominant breed, **ask your vet to look up the breed-specific recommendation** rather than defaulting to a generic age.

## Females: the "before or after first heat" question

For small/medium females, many vets still recommend spaying **before the first heat** (~5–6 months) because it dramatically reduces lifetime mammary-cancer risk and eliminates pyometra and heat cycles. For **large/giant females**, that benefit is weighed against the joint-health reasons to wait. This trade-off is exactly why it's an individualized decision.

## The procedure & recovery (what to expect)

- **Beforehand:** pre-anesthetic exam and often bloodwork; fasting per your clinic's instructions.
- **The surgery:** done under general anesthesia. Spaying (ovariohysterectomy or ovariectomy) is more involved than neutering; both are routine.
- **Recovery:** usually **10–14 days** of **restricted activity** — no running, jumping, or rough play while the incision heals. An **e-collar or recovery suit** prevents licking. Watch the incision for **redness, swelling, discharge, or opening**, and call the clinic if you see them or if your dog is very lethargic, vomiting, or off food.
- **Retained baby teeth** or an **umbilical hernia**, if present, are often addressed during the same anesthesia — ask.

!!! warning "Caring for an intact dog while you wait"
    If you're delaying surgery for a large breed, you're responsible for
    preventing accidental litters. Intact females come into heat roughly every
    6 months (bleeding and attractive to males for ~2–3 weeks); intact males
    may roam, mark, and mount. Plan for secure containment and supervision
    during this window.
