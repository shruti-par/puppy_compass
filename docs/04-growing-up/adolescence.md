# Surviving adolescence

Somewhere around **6 months**, many owners panic: the sweet, biddable puppy they spent months training suddenly acts like they've never heard the word "come," ignores known cues, gets bouncier and bolder, and seems to push every boundary. Congratulations — you have a teenager. This is normal, it's temporary, and how you handle it shapes the adult dog.

## What's actually going on

Adolescence (roughly **6–18 months**, longer in big breeds) is a period of major brain rewiring and hormonal change. Just like human teens, dogs this age have surging confidence, big feelings, and an impulse-control system that hasn't caught up. The training isn't *gone* — it's temporarily drowned out by hormones and distraction.

This is, sadly, the **most common age for dogs to be rehomed or surrendered**, precisely because owners mistake a developmental phase for a permanent personality. Knowing it's a phase is half the battle.

## What you'll likely see

- **"Selective deafness"** — suddenly ignoring cues they knew cold.
- **Boundary testing** — counter-surfing, leash-pulling, jumping, re-testing rules.
- **A second fear period** — new (or previously fine) things become scary for a while.
- **More energy and more independence** — wandering further, big play.
- **Hormonal behaviors in intact dogs** — marking, mounting, roaming, interest in other dogs.

## How to get through it well

1. **Don't take it personally, and don't give up the training.** Quietly keep rewarding the behaviors you want. Consistency through adolescence is what makes the adult dog reliable.
2. **Go back to basics.** Treat recall and manners like you're re-teaching them — higher-value rewards, easier situations, fewer distractions, then build back up.
3. **Use management.** A **long line** for recall safety, gates and crates to prevent rehearsed bad habits, and a tidy environment so there's less to get into.
4. **Meet the energy.** More **mental** work (sniffing, puzzle feeders, training games, scent work) tires an adolescent better than just more physical exercise — and protects growing joints.
5. **Enforce rest.** Overtired adolescents are impulsive and mouthy, just like overtired puppies. Naps still matter.
6. **Keep socializing.** Positive experiences with people and dogs continue to pay off; the second fear period is a reason to be *gentle*, not to stop.

!!! warning "When it's more than teenage attitude"
    There's a difference between normal boundary-testing and genuine **fear,
    reactivity, or aggression.** If your dog is showing real fear responses,
    growling/snapping out of fear, intense reactivity to dogs or people, or
    severe separation distress, loop in your vet and a **certified positive-
    reinforcement trainer or veterinary behaviorist** sooner rather than later.
    Early intervention is far easier than waiting.

!!! tip "Size note"
    Adolescence lasts **longer in large and giant breeds** — a Great Dane may
    be a goofy teenager well past two years. Match your patience (and your
    [spay/neuter timing](spay-neuter.md) and exercise plans) to that longer
    runway. Small breeds generally settle sooner.

The dog on the other side of adolescence is the one you've been building all along. Keep going — it's worth it.
